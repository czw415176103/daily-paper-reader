# Abstract 

We propose an unsupervised learning framework for calibrating a physics-augmented neural network (PANN) for small-strain viscoelasticity via full-field data. It only requires quantities that are directly accessible in real experiments for training, namely global reaction forces and surface displacements. The underlying PANN is embedded in the generalized standard materials theory, in which two scalar-valued potentials render the constitutive model thermodynamically consistent by construction, while invariant-based representations of the free energy and the dual dissipation potential additionally ensure material symmetry. Considering a thin specimen under the plane stress assumption, we formulate a constrained optimization problem based on the equilibrium gap method in combination with quasi-Newton optimizers and automatic differentiation. Thereby, the unknown out-of-plane strain follows from the plane stress condition and the evolution of the internal variables is captured by an implicit time integration scheme. The resulting system of nonlinear equations is solved via a local Newton iteration at quadrature point and time step. To drastically reduce the computational cost of training, the backward adjoint method is employed to compute the gradient of the target loss, instead of backpropagating through all Newton iteration steps. The proposed framework is demonstrated for synthetic data, including noisy displacements and forces, showing excellent agreement across a wide range of deformation rates and load paths. 

Keywords viscoelasticity · generalized standard materials · physics-augmented neural networks · full-field data · equilibrium gap method 

# 1 Introduction 

Over the past century, extensive research has established the physical and mathematical principles that constitutive models should obey (Haupt, 2000, Holzapfel, 2000, Šilhavý, 1997), giving rise to a wide range of so-called classical constitutive models . However, when applied to materials that exhibit highly nonlinear behavior, such models may be too restrictive to ac-curately fit the data and may need to be modified for each new set of experimental data. To address these shortcomings, 

machine learning approaches—particularly neural networks (NNs) —have emerged as powerful tools for constitutive mod-eling (Dornheim et al., 2023, Fuhg et al., 2024), offering the flexibility to capture complex material responses while automat-ing the modeling process.

In the following, we first give an overview of neural constitutive modeling for both elasticity and inelasticity. Building on this, we then review the most common strategies for calibrating such data-driven material models from full-field data. 

1.1 Literature review on neural constitutive modeling 

In their seminal work from the early 1990s, Ghaboussi et al. (1991) were the first to employ NNs as constitutive models. Inspired by this work, a number of follow-up studies were conducted. However, after these initial steps, the field was not actively pursued for quite some time afterward. With the recent rise of machine learning and gains in computational efficiency, however, a variety of data-driven techniques have rapidly gained momentum in mechanics, as reviewed in (Bock et al., 2019, Dornheim et al., 2023, Fuhg et al., 2024). arXiv:2609.03645v1 [cs.CE] 3 Sep 2026 Calibration of neural viscoelastic models via full-field data A Preprint 

A significant step forward in neural constitutive modeling—and in scientific machine learning more broadly—is the integration of fundamental physical concepts, variously termed physics-informed (Raissi et al., 2019), mechanics-informed (As’ad & Farhat, 2023), physics-augmented (Klein et al., 2024, Linden et al., 2023), physics-based (Aldakheel et al., 2025), physics-constrained (Kalina et al., 2023), or thermodynamics-based 

(Masi et al., 2021). Physical knowledge can be embedded in two ways (Rosenkranz et al., 2023): strongly , through network architectures tailored to the problem (Kalina et al., 2022, Linka et al., 2021), or weakly , through problem-specific loss functions used during training (Geiger et al., 2025, Masi et al., 2021). As demonstrated in (Fuhg et al., 2023, Linden et al., 2023, Masi et al., 2021), such models substantially enhance extrapolation capability. In the following, we provide a brief overview of the current state of the art in neural constitutive modeling. Numerous works model elasticity with NNs, where the most common approach is to use architectures with the hyperelastic potential as output and invariants as inputs, e.g., (Bahmani & Sun, 2024, Benady et al., 2024a, Fuhg et al., 2022, Klein et al., 2021, Linden et al., 2023, Linka et al., 2021, Peirlinck et al., 2024, Taç et al., 2024). Direct calibration from stress-strain tuples is enabled via Sobolev training proposed by Czarnecki et al. (2017) and adopted to mechanics by Vlassis et al. (2020), in which the loss function involves the gradient of the energy with respect to the deformation. Many works further employ 

polyconvex NNs (Bahmani & Sun, 2024, Chen & Guilleminot, 2022, Dammaß et al., 2025, Geuken et al., 2025, Jadoon et al., 2025a, Klein et al., 2021, Taç et al., 2024, Tac et al., 2022, Vijayakumaran et al., 2025) among many others, which enhance extrapolation (Kalina et al., 2024, Linden et al., 2023) and guarantee rank-one convexity and thus ellipticity (Schröder et al., 2010). This is most commonly realized via the fully input-convex neural networks (FICNNs) of Amos et al. (2017). Besides neural elasticity models, a large number of works on 

inelasticity have also emerged in recent years. A key require-ment for any inelastic material model is compatibility with the Clausius-Duhem inequality , which demands non-negative dissipation rates 𝐷 ≥ 0. A first group of data-driven models 

enforces this through tailored loss terms. An influential example is the thermodynamics-based artificial neural network (TANN) 

by Masi et al. (2021), which realizes neural inelasticity. Despite its innovative character, the approach has two drawbacks: ther-modynamic consistency is only weakly enforced , and knowledge of the internal variables is required for training, which was solved later via integration for constitutive equations (Masi & Einav, 2024). A related approach for inelasticity is presented in (He & Chen, 2022): in contrast to the TANN, the internal state variables capturing the path-dependency are inferred from the hidden state of a recurrent neural network (RNN). Another class of neural inelasticity models follows the structure of classical elasto-plastic formulations more closely, e.g., (Malik et al., 2021, Settgast et al., 2020, Vlassis & Sun, 2021a,b), but does not guarantee 𝐷 ≥ 0 by construction. This limitation is overcome in (Fuhg et al., 2023, Meyer & Ekre, 2023), where 

thermodynamically consistent neural elasto-plasticity models 

for small deformations are obtained, e.g., by modeling the dissipation or plastic potential via FICNNs (Fuhg et al., 2023). In both cases, training requires no explicitly prescribed internal variables; instead, the return-mapping problem is solved within each optimization step. Extensions to finite deformations (Boes et al., 2024, Jadoon et al., 2025b) and, most recently, to finite-strain viscoelastic-plastic behavior with combined nonlinear kinematic and isotropic hardening (van der Velden et al., 2026) build on these ideas. Beyond elasto-plasticity, several groups have also carried out extensive research on neural viscoelasticity models grounded in a rigorous physical framework. An important contribution is that of Huang et al. (2022), embedded in the generalized stan-dard material (GSM) framework 1: thermodynamic consistency is ensured through a dissipation potential that is convex with respect to the internal variables’ rates, normalized, and station-ary at zero rates—or, equivalently, through a dual dissipation potential with analogous properties that depends on the thermo-dynamically conjugated forces. Meanwhile, several data-driven approaches building up on a small-strain GSM framework exist, e.g., (Flaschel et al., 2025, Rosenkranz et al., 2024, Shi & Luo, 2026). In contrast to (Huang et al., 2022), these require no prescribed internal variables during training—only their number must be specified. As discussed and benchmarked by Rosenkranz et al. (2024), two main strategies exist: solving a 

constrained optimization problem subject to the time-discretized evolution equation(s), which entails time integration over the en-tire sequence, or employing an auxiliary network —either FNN (As’ad & Farhat, 2023) or RNN (Rosenkranz et al., 2024)—to 

provide the internal variable(s) during training . Notably, in the latter case the auxiliary network is used only during training; afterwards, the evolution equation is solved in prediction mode. Several works also address the more general case of finite-strain viscoelasticity . A transversely isotropic model based on the multiplicative split of the deformation gradient and using neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs) is proposed in (Taç et al., 2023), while a related GSM-based model assuming in-compressibility and the multiplicative decomposition represents the thermodynamic potentials via FICNNs (Kalina et al., 2026). Isotropic models with a multiplicative split and a co-rotational formulation are presented in (Holthusen et al., 2024a,b, 2026). Here, Holthusen et al. (2026) introduce a dual dissipation po-tential that ensures thermodynamic consistency under a less restrictive convexity requirement—the potential needs only be convex, stationary, and normalized in a modified invariant set rather than with respect to the thermodynamic forces themselves. An extension to transversely isotropic viscoelasticity follows in (Holthusen & Kuhl, 2026). A further GSM-based finite-strain model, which does not assume the multiplicative split, is given in (As’ad & Farhat, 2023, 2026). Other thermodynamically consistent models avoid the two-potential framework altogether, including formulations based on deformation rates (Upadhyay et al., 2026), the NN-based model

1.2 Literature review on full-field calibration techniques 

Data for calibrating neural constitutive models can be obtained in various ways. Depending on their origin and spatial resolution, the sources fall into three categories: lower-scale simulations ,

conventional experiments , and full-field experiments . We focus on the latter in the following. Beyond global quantities such as reaction forces , full-field setups provide spatially resolved displacement fields via full-field measurement techniques . Unlike conventional experiments, the specimens are deliberately designed to produce inhomogeneous local fields , thereby shifting constitutive modeling from a limited-data to a large-data regime (Fuhg et al., 2024, Pierron, 2023). However, stress-strain pairs cannot be determined directly in this setting: while inhomogeneous displacement fields are accessible through camera systems with digital image correlation (DIC) in two dimensions (Pierron & Grédiac, 2020) or in-situ computed tomography (CT) combined with digital volume correlation (DVC) in three dimensions (Lenoir et al., 2007), the corresponding stress fields are not. Identifying the model parameters therefore requires solving an inverse problem .Numerous methods for inverse parameter identification have been developed over the past decades; comprehensive overviews are given in (Avril et al., 2008, Chen et al., 2025, Römer et al., 2024, Roux & Hild, 2020). In (Mahnken & Stein, 1996, Sakaridis et al., 2024), for instance, the parameters of classical models are identified by iteratively running finite element simulations of the experiment and minimizing the discrepancy between measured and simulated responses—an approach known as finite element model updating (FEMU) 

(Avril et al., 2008, Römer et al., 2024). Further methods tailored specifically to inverse problems include the virtual fields method (VFM) (Grédiac, 1989, Pierron & Grédiac, 2012), the equilibrium gap method (EGM) (Claire et al., 2004) 3, and the modified constitutive relation error (mCRE) method. 

Remark 1. For inverse methods that obtain displacement fields from forward simulations, such as FEMU or the mCRE, full-field displacement measurements are not strictly required: the loss function can be formulated purely in terms of global quan-tities such as reaction forces. When available, however, local displacement data is often included to improve identification accuracy. In contrast, the EGM and VFM rely directly on full-field displacement data over the entire domain to compute deformation measures and, subsequently, stresses, so that no

Recently, inverse identification techniques have been transferred to a data-driven context, and new approaches have emerged. The EUCLID framework (efficient unsupervised constitutive law identification & discovery) by Flaschel et al. (2021, 2023), for instance, extends the EGM/VFM by simultaneously determining parameters and suitable constitutive models from a catalog via sparse regression, with experimental validation on 2D DIC data given in (Abbasi et al., 2026). NN-EUCLID (Thakolkaran et al., 2022) replaces the model catalog with a neural constitutive model, while CANN-EUCLID (Alheit et al., 2026) calibrates a constitutive artificial neural network (CANN) following Linka & Kuhl (2023). Related strategies discover neural yield surfaces (Moon et al., 2026) and train Kolmogorov-Arnold networks (KANs) (Thakolkaran et al., 2025). Applications include 3D displacement fields from bulge inflation (Meng et al., 2025) and 3D DVC data from a printed structure (Bourdyot et al., 2026). Along the same lines, Ferreira & Bessa (2026) calibrate various neural elasto-plastic models, including RNN-based ones, via EGM, whereas Lourenço et al. (2024) integrate RNN models into the VFM. An approach combining the statistical finite element method (stat-FEM) with EUCLID is introduced in (Knauf Narouie et al., 2026). Thereby, the integration of the stat-FEM reduces sensitivity to noise and sparse displacement data. Further approaches address anisotropic elasticity (Li et al., 2026) and heterogeneous samples (Chaurasiya et al., 2026, Shi et al., 2025, Taç et al., 2026). The NN-mCRE approach extends the mCRE concept, replacing the classical constitutive model with a physics-augmented neural network (PANN) (Benady et al., 2024a,b). PANNs are also combined with FEMU (Wu et al., 2025), as are spline-based constitutive models (Wiesheier et al., 2024, 2026) and CANNs for incompressible hyperelasticity (Knipper et al., 2026). In the same manner, neural elasto-plastic (Gavris & Sun, 2025) and neural cohesive zone models (Gavris & Sun, 2026) are calibrated. 

1.3 Objectives and contributions of this work 

As discussed in the literature overview given above, numerous 

neural viscoelasticity models exist that combine modern ma-chine learning methods with a sound physical basis. Among these, NN models that employ invariants and are embedded into the GSM framework appear very promising, as they enforce material symmetry and thermodynamic consistency by con-struction. However, irrespective of the specific architecture, the vast majority of these approaches are calibrated in a supervised 

manner, i.e., they rely on stress-strain tuples that need to be derived via suitable assumptions from standard experiments, e.g., uniaxial tension tests with dogbone specimens. There are only a few studies that calibrate highly flexible yet physically consistent data-driven models such as PANNs for 

viscoelasticity in a fully unsupervised manner using full-field data , thereby enabling access to an extensive database. Such a model should not only maintain thermodynamic consistency, objectivity, and material symmetry in its design, but also capture 3Calibration of neural viscoelastic models via full-field data A Preprint 

the evolution of the internal variables using a suitable time integration scheme without specifying them a priori. We therefore present such a framework for the small-strain setting in this article. To this end, we adopt the PANN for isotropic small-strain viscoelasticity introduced in (Rosenkranz et al., 2024), which builds on the GSM framework and employs two scalar-valued potentials, rendering the model thermodynam-ically consistent by construction. Considering a thin specimen under a plane stress assumption , we formulate a constrained optimization problem based on the EGM in combination with quasi-Newton optimizers and automatic differentiation. The evolution of the internal variables is captured via an implicit time integration scheme. Together with the plane stress con-dition 𝜎 33 = 0, from which the unknown out-of-plane strain 

𝜀 33 is determined, this yields a system of nonlinear equations that is solved simultaneously by a local Newton iteration at each time step and quadrature point. To drastically reduce the computational cost of training, the backward adjoint method is employed to compute the gradient of the target loss. The organization of the remaining paper is as follows: In Sect. 2, the continuum mechanical basics and the underlying GSM framework for viscoelasticity are presented. After this, a PANN framework for the description of the potentials is introduced in Sect. 3. This is followed by Sect. 4 introducing the EGM that is used for unsupervised training of the PANN. The developed approach is exemplarily applied to several examples in Sect. 5. After a discussion of the results, the paper is closed by concluding remarks and an outlook to necessary future work in Sect. 6. In this work we use the notation specified in App. A.

# 4 Equilibrium gap method 

As discussed in the Sect. 1.2, numerous approaches are available for the calibration of constitutive models via full-field data. In the present work, we adopt a special case of the VFM, the EGM (Claire et al., 2004), to identify the material model’s parameter in an unsupervised manner. The parameters, collected in 𝜽 ,are in our case weights and biases of the FICNNs introduced in Sect. 3. The methodology is therefore similar to the NN-EUCLID framework by Thakolkaran et al. (2022). In our case, however, we consider a neural viscoelastic model instead of a hyperelastic one. Data for the EGM can be collected via experiments with full-field displacement measurement (Abbasi et al., 2026, Jailin et al., 2024), where the displacements are typically obtained via 6Calibration of neural viscoelastic models via full-field data A Preprint 

optical techniques such as DIC. In the EGM, the displacement is required not just at a few discrete locations but at a sufficiently large number of points distributed across the region of interest (ROI), cf. Remark 1. Since in real setups DIC only allows to observe displacements on the sample surface—and in many setups only on one side—we will restrict ourselves to thin samples in the following. In this case, the ROI is therefore a 2D domain denoted by 𝛺 ⊂ R2 with boundary 𝜕𝛺 , i.e., the closure is cl 𝛺 := 𝛺 ∪ 𝜕𝛺 .We are working on the basis of the following assumptions and assume that the data listed below is known after the raw data has been preprocessed: (I) The specimen is thin compared with its in-plane char-acteristic length, so that a plane stress state is assumed throughout the entire domain. Remark 8. It is known from the literature that training inelastic neural models via ODE-constrained optimization, as done in this work, can fail whenever an unfavorable set of the trainable parameters causes quantities such as stresses to drift toward infinity, resulting in NaN values, cf. (Holthusen & Kuhl, 2026, p. 20) or (Kalina et al., 2026, Remark 10). The SLSQP optimizer used here is comparatively robust against such scenarios. If the optimizer receives the value NaN, it can switch to a stable region of the parameters 𝜽 ∈ P 𝜃 . Only if NaN values occur in the very first training epoch does SLSQP terminate immediately. One way to prevent NaNs in this initial epoch is to choose the trainable parameters such that the initial relaxation times take on meaningful values (Kalina et al., 2026, Remark 9). For the cases considered here, however, it was sufficient to restrict the initialization of the weights to the interval [0, 0.1].

5.3.2 Results 

As in the case of ideal data, the PANN was trained using ten initializations for each of the three datasets: noisy uniaxial, noisy biaxial, and the combination of both. For the noisy data, training terminated early for some of these initializations, because the least-squares method used to com-pute the update in the Newton scheme in the forward pass encountered an ill-conditioned coefficient matrix. In total, cali-bration completed successfully for five out of ten initializations in the case of the noisy uniaxial dataset, and for eight out of ten each in the case of the noisy biaxial and the combined noisy datasets. As only a limited number of calibrations can be executed, early termination could in principle also occur for the ideal data considered previously, even though it was not observed there. Among the calibrations that completed successfully, the PANN with the smallest loss was again selected as the best-performing model for each of the three datasets. These trained PANNs were subsequently employed in a finite element simulation of the validation boundary value problem shown in Fig. 3. The resulting stress component 𝜎 PANN,noisy 11 is compared in Fig. 7 to the GT values 𝜎 GT 11 obtained from the linear viscoelastic model. Compared to the case of ideal data, the absolute stress difference is now noticeably larger for all three trained PANNs. The best agreement is obtained for the PANN trained on both noisy uniaxial and biaxial data, followed by the PANN trained solely on noisy biaxial data, and lastly by the PANN trained solely on noisy uniaxial data. We assume that this ordering is governed more by the specific initialization than by different training data. Furthermore, as expected, the largest stress differences occur near the boundaries, where spatial displacement gradients are high. Aside from this, the overall stress prediction remains reasonably accurate. The remaining stress components predicted by the three PANNs exhibit a similar level of error when compared to the reference values. 

# 6 Conclusion 

In this work, an unsupervised learning framework for calibrating a PANN for small-strain viscoelasticity from full-field data is proposed. The approach relies solely on quantities accessible in real experiments— global reaction forces and surface displace-ments —and formulates an optimization problem based on the EGM that is constrained under a plane stress assumption and additionally by the evolution equations of internal variables. The underlying PANN is embedded in the GSM framework, combining invariant-based representations of the free energy and the DDP, such that thermodynamic consistency and material symmetry hold by construction. To reduce the training cost, the gradient of the target loss is evaluated via the backward adjoint method . The framework shows excellent agreement with synthetically generated data and reliably recovers the material response even in the presence of added artificial noise. In summary, the presented framework demonstrates that a ther-modynamically consistent viscoelastic PANN can be calibrated using only data that is measurable in real experiments, thereby closing the gap between physics-augmented neural inelasticity modeling and full-field experimental characterization. Its key advantage lies in the generality of the applied framework, which can be transferred in a straightforward manner to different con-stitutive settings and thus enable a systematic, largely automated approach to material modeling. Several extensions are planned for the future. Natural next steps include the calibration with real experimental data (Abbasi et al., 2026, Wiesheier et al., 2026), the extension to finite strains (Holthusen & Kuhl, 2026, Kalina et al., 2026), and the transfer of the approach to further inelastic material classes such as elasto-plasticity (Fuhg et al., 2023, Meyer & Ekre, 2023).
